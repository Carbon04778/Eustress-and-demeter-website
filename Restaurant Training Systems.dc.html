Training systems that survive the person who built them

Ask most restaurants how they train and the answer is that new hires follow someone experienced for a few days. That is not training, it is exposure. It is also why standards drift a little with every hire and nobody can point to when it started.

The short answer

A restaurant training system has three parts: written standards, structured station training with a defined path, and a test that certifies the person can perform to standard unsupervised. Shadowing fails because it transmits whatever the trainer happens to do that day, including their shortcuts. The measure of a training system is not hours delivered but time to competence and how consistent that competence is across trainers.

01

Why shadowing fails

Following an experienced employee feels efficient and costs nothing to organize, which is why it is the default almost everywhere. The problems are structural.

It transmits shortcuts along with standards. Every experienced cook has adaptations. Some are improvements; many are drift. A new hire cannot tell the difference and absorbs both.

Quality depends entirely on the trainer. Two new hires trained by different people learn measurably different jobs. Multiply this across locations and consistency becomes impossible.

Nothing is verified. The new hire is declared trained when the days elapse, not when they can perform. Nobody tests, so nobody knows.

It is unrepeatable. When the experienced trainer leaves, the training capability leaves with them, which is exactly the dependency that prevents growth.

02

The three components

A working system needs all three. Any two produces something that looks like training and does not behave like it.

  1. 01

    Written standards

    What correct looks like, documented per task, with photographs. Without this, training has no fixed reference and the standard becomes whoever taught you. Start with the SOP guide.

  2. 02

    A structured path

    A defined sequence of stations with expected duration at each. The trainee and their manager both know where they are and what comes next.

  3. 03

    Certification by test

    A practical assessment at the end of each station, marked against the written standard by someone other than the trainer. Passing is a decision, not a date.

03

Designing the station path

Order stations by dependency, not by convenience. Each should build on the last, and nobody should work a station uncertified.

  • Day one is orientation, not a stationBrand, standards, safety, and why the business does things the way it does. Skipping this produces employees who follow rules they do not understand and abandon them under pressure.
  • Start where mistakes are cheapestPrep or dish before the line. Competence and confidence build somewhere errors are recoverable.
  • One station at a timeCertify before moving on. Partial competence across four stations is less useful than full competence at one.
  • Define expected durationTwo shifts on this station, three on that one. Deviation is a signal worth examining rather than an inconvenience.
  • Name the trainersNot whoever is on shift. A small group of designated trainers, themselves trained to train, is what makes output consistent.
  • Test practicallyProduce the item, execute the procedure, handle the situation. Written tests measure recall; practical tests measure capability.
04

Manager development

Most groups train hourly staff reasonably and promote managers with nothing at all, then wonder why the new general manager struggles.

A manager needs a different curriculum: reading a P&L, deploying labor against forecast, holding a difficult conversation, investigating variance, running a shift meeting, and handling a guest recovery that has escalated. None of that is learned on a station.

The practical approach is a defined certification path with the same structure as station training: written standards, a sequence, and a test. It should take months rather than days, and it should be running before you need the manager, not after.

This matters increasingly with scale. Past fifteen locations you cannot recruit general managers externally at the rate you open; you have to produce them.

05

Measuring whether it works

Training is easy to feel good about and hard to evaluate. Four measures make it concrete.

  • Time to competenceDays from hire to certification, by station. Should be consistent. Wide variance means the trainers, not the trainees, are inconsistent.
  • First-attempt pass rateIf everyone passes first time, the test is too easy. If many fail, the training is inadequate. Both are useful signals.
  • Ninety-day retentionThe clearest indicator of onboarding quality. People leave early when they feel incompetent, which is a training failure rather than a hiring one.
  • Variance after certificationFood variance and ticket times attributable to newly certified staff. If they rise, certification is not testing the right things.

If everybody passes the test first time, you do not have a test.

06

Keeping it current

Training material decays exactly like SOPs, and for the same reasons. A menu change that does not reach the training program means new hires learn the previous version.

Tie training material to the SOP system so a change updates both. Name an owner. Review quarterly for anything food-related and twice a year for the rest.

One further discipline: recertify existing staff annually on food safety and on the top ten items. It is inexpensive, and it catches drift among long-tenured employees, which is where drift usually starts.

FAQ

Common questions

How long should restaurant training take?

For a full-service concept, one to two weeks to competence on a first station and longer for the full path. What matters more than the total is that duration is consistent between trainees, because wide variance indicates the process depends on who is teaching.

Who should be a trainer?

A small designated group, chosen for ability to teach rather than seniority, and trained in how to train. Using whoever is on shift is what makes output inconsistent across locations.

Should we pay a training premium?

Paying designated trainers a differential is common and generally worthwhile. It signals that training is a real responsibility and makes it easier to hold trainers to a standard.

How do we train managers rather than staff?

A separate certification path covering financial literacy, labor deployment, difficult conversations, variance investigation and guest recovery. It should run over months, use the same structure of standards, sequence and test, and start before you need the person.
